*Updated October 2026*

What does a medical system notice when it begins with a person's way of living?

This question kept returning as I read about Tibetan medicine. A discussion of digestion would lead to the seasons; a discussion of distress would lead to bodily movement; an account of medicines would lead to the physician's intentions. The connections were more interesting than the familiar claim that traditional medicine is “holistic.” I wanted to understand how those connections actually work.

My starting point is reading, rather than clinical experience or travel. I have tried to keep three questions in view: **What does this tradition say? What makes its reasoning intelligible? What evidence supports a particular claim?** They overlap, but each requires a different kind of answer.

The result is an introduction to *Sowa Rigpa*, usually translated as the “science of healing.” I am most interested in its account of the relationship between a body, a mind, and a life—and in how to take that account seriously while remaining precise about what we know.

## 1. A living tradition with a structured curriculum

Tibetan medicine is a learned medical tradition practiced across Tibet and other Himalayan regions, with communities of practitioners elsewhere. Its physicians are often called *amchi*. Historical scholarship describes substantial exchanges with neighboring medical traditions, particularly Indian medicine, alongside the development of distinct Tibetan texts and practices. It makes more sense to approach this history as an accumulation of translations, arguments, and clinical knowledge than as the discovery of a single ancient secret. [Gurmet, 2004](https://nopr.niscpr.res.in/handle/123456789/9345)

Its central textual framework is the *Gyüshi* (*rgyud bzhi*), the **Four Tantras**. The four sections move from an outline of the system to explanations of the body, instructions for treating disorders, and further material on diagnosis and medicines. The tradition presents these teachings within a sacred Buddhist setting. Historical accounts place the formation of the foundational text in the medieval period, particularly the twelfth century. Sacred attribution and the historical development of a text answer different questions. [Men-Tsee-Khang, *Tibetan Medical Treatise*](https://mentseekhang.org/tibetan-medical-treatise/); [Hofer, *Medical Paintings*](https://rubinmuseum.org/projecthimalayanart/essays/desi-sanggye-gyatsos-medical-paintings/)

An especially revealing feature is the use of **trees as teaching devices**. Roots, trunks, branches, and leaves organize physiology, disease, diagnosis, and treatment into a structure students can memorize. The medical paintings associated with Desi Sangye Gyatso's seventeenth-century *Blue Beryl* commentary turn this structure into a visual curriculum. Their attention extends to ordinary bodies, foods, and daily activities. [Hofer, *Medical Paintings*](https://rubinmuseum.org/projecthimalayanart/essays/desi-sanggye-gyatsos-medical-paintings/)

I find this a useful entrance into the subject. A tree makes relationships visible without making every branch identical. To understand the system, we need to follow the branches.

## 2. The body's basic vocabulary

### Five elements, three nyépa

Sowa Rigpa describes bodies, foods, medicines, and their surroundings through five elements: **earth, water, fire, wind, and space**. These are traditional categories for understanding qualities and processes. Reading them as entries in a chemical inventory would miss their role in the theory. [Gurmet, 2004](https://nopr.niscpr.res.in/handle/123456789/9345)

The next essential term is *nyépa* (*nyes pa*). The three nyépa are commonly translated as wind, bile, and phlegm. “Humors” is another common translation, although it can suggest that these are simply three bodily liquids. In the traditional account, they organize functions that sustain life; when disturbed, they also organize explanations of illness. Practitioner Pasang Yonten Arya's introductory teaching presents health and disease through this relationship between normal function and disturbance. [Arya, *Short Introduction*](https://www.tibetanmedicine-edu.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/TibetanMedicineD1.pdf)

| Traditional term | Common English rendering | Functions emphasized in the traditional model |
|---|---|---|
| *rlung* | Wind | Movement, respiration, mental activity, and the movement of substances through the body |
| *tripa* (*mkhris pa*) | Bile | Heat, digestion, and transformation |
| *béken* (*bad kan*) | Phlegm | Stability, cohesion, and aspects of nourishment and bodily structure |

*Table 1. A simplified orientation to the three nyépa, following practitioner teaching. These are overlapping functional categories, rather than three isolated organs or measurable chemicals.* [Arya, *Short Introduction*](https://www.tibetanmedicine-edu.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/TibetanMedicineD1.pdf)

The word “balance” needs care here. It does not imply three equal quantities. A useful way to read the model is to ask whether functions and qualities are appropriate to the person and circumstances. Calling something balanced supplies a theoretical judgment; it does not, by itself, supply a laboratory measurement.

### What happens to food?

Digestion offers a more concrete view. The *Explanatory Tantra* describes **digestive heat**, often rendered *médrö* (*me drod*), as central to the transformation of food. Wind, bile, and phlegm cooperate in this process. Nutritional essence sustains the bodily constituents; digestion therefore connects eating with the maintenance of the whole body. The text uses the familiar logic of cooking to explain transformation. [*Root and Explanatory Tantras*, chapter 5](https://djvu.online/file/6GSwgaPT6riL0)

The system distinguishes seven bodily constituents—nutritional essence, blood, muscle, fat, bone, marrow, and reproductive fluid—and three principal waste products: stool, urine, and sweat. These categories belong to an account of nourishment, maintenance, and elimination. [Gurmet, 2004](https://nopr.niscpr.res.in/handle/123456789/9345)

This helps explain why advice about food is central to the tradition. If the body is continually sustained through transformation, eating is already part of the therapeutic field. A medicine is introduced into a process that is also being shaped by meals, habits, and the person's condition.

It is tempting to translate digestive heat straight into enzymes, metabolism, or the microbiome. I would resist that shortcut. A comparison becomes useful when it identifies a specific question to investigate. A shared concern with digestion does not establish that two explanatory systems refer to the same mechanism.

## 3. Wind, emotion, and Buddhist ideas about illness

### Is rlung the same as qi?

*Rlung* is the closest place to begin this comparison, but it deserves to be understood in its own terms. Historian Ronit Yoeli-Tlalim describes a concept that connects bodily motion with mental and verbal activity. She discusses comparisons with Chinese *qi* and Greek *pneuma*, while emphasizing the difficulty of translation. Medical wind and the subtle winds of tantric practice also occur in related but distinguishable contexts. [Yoeli-Tlalim, 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3384002/)

A comparison can therefore orient the reader: several traditions developed vocabularies that relate breath, movement, and life. It cannot justify replacing every occurrence of *rlung* with “energy,” or identifying it with oxygen, nerves, or electricity. Each substitution silently adds claims that the original term does not necessarily make.

What interests me most is how wind crosses a boundary that English often draws between a physical complaint and an emotional one. Accounts based on interviews with Tibetan physicians in Nepal describe wind disorders through interacting bodily, emotional, and situational factors. Stress and mental distress can be discussed within the same framework as movement and digestion. [LMU TransTibMed, *Wind Disorders*](https://www.en.transtibmed.ethnologie.uni-muenchen.de/tibetan-medicine/diagnoses-tib-med/wind-disorders/index.html)

This vocabulary gives a person a way to connect experiences that might otherwise feel scattered. Whether a particular diagnosis accurately predicts illness or treatment response is a further question, requiring its own investigation.

### Why do attachment, anger, and ignorance appear in medicine?

The relevant Buddhist framework is the **three poisons**: attachment or craving, aversion or hatred, and ignorance or delusion. Traditional accounts associate them with wind, bile, and phlegm respectively. Here “ignorance” refers to a Buddhist understanding of misapprehension, rather than a lack of education. Pride and doubt appear in broader Buddhist classifications, but should not be inserted into this particular threefold correspondence. [Anders, 2021](https://www.transtibmed.ethnologie.uni-muenchen.de/publikationen/history-mental-health-tibmed.pdf)

There is also a distinction between **underlying causes and immediate conditions**. The three poisons belong to a deep account of vulnerability to suffering. Diet, behavior, seasons, and other circumstances help explain how particular disturbances arise. The tradition contains spiritual causal categories as well as everyday observations; omitting the former would make the system easier to modernize but harder to understand on its own terms. [Yoeli-Tlalim, 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3384002/)

I read this as an attempt to place illness within a larger theory of human life. It raises an ethical difficulty, however: causal language can become blame. A humane discussion must not turn a person's illness into evidence of moral failure. Explaining a traditional doctrine does not require accepting that a patient's suffering reveals the quality of their character.

The distinction also protects scientific clarity. An observation that distress accompanies bodily symptoms is one claim. A Buddhist explanation of why beings are vulnerable to suffering is another. The first does not automatically verify the second.

## 4. From an account of the body to a clinical encounter

Tibetan medical diagnosis traditionally combines **inspection, touch, and questioning**. Inspection includes the tongue and urine; touch includes pulse examination. Questions address symptoms and the circumstances of the patient's life. The findings are interpreted together within the medical framework. [Men-Tsee-Khang, *Tibetan Medical Treatise*](https://mentseekhang.org/tibetan-medical-treatise/)

An important distinction is the difference between a method being elaborate and a method being validated. A pulse tradition can involve substantial training and a sophisticated vocabulary. To assess a specific diagnostic claim, I would still want to know whether practitioners agree, whether their judgments predict independently assessed outcomes, and whether using those judgments improves care.

An actual case record makes the breadth of the encounter clearer. In Theresia Hofer's translation of an amchi's records, one patient is described through a troubled, trembling heart, difficulty sleeping, and difficulty passing stools. The recorded treatment combines medicinal formulations with moxibustion, an application of heat. Emotional experience, sleep, and elimination appear in one clinical account. The record documents a physician's assessment and treatment; it supplies no controlled evidence that the treatment succeeded. [Hofer, *Case Records*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK546478/table/ch7.tab1/)

What I take from this example is a way of organizing attention. A reader can see which experiences enter the encounter together. The next questions concern how the physician distinguishes possible patterns, chooses among interventions, and assesses what happens afterward.

The treatment framework has four broad domains: **diet, behavior, medicines, and external therapies**. Contemporary Men-Tsee-Khang descriptions include dietary and lifestyle counseling, medicinal preparations, and procedures such as massage, heat applications, and medicinal bathing. Particular choices depend on the patient and the disorder. [Men-Tsee-Khang, *FAQ*](https://mentseekhang.org/faq/)

Lum medicinal bathing is a useful example of how treatment also exists within community life. It includes practices involving natural hot springs, herbal water, or steam. UNESCO's description places it within knowledge transmitted through households, practitioners, religious activities, and medical education. Its 2018 heritage inscription recognizes a living cultural practice; that recognition is not a clinical efficacy assessment. [UNESCO, *Lum Medicinal Bathing*](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/01386)

Together, these branches make “holistic” more concrete. The system can direct attention to a person's routines, surroundings, symptoms, and relationships between bodily functions. The quality of that attention and the effectiveness of a specific treatment remain related but separable questions.

## 5. How medicines are supposed to work

### A language of qualities

Traditional pharmacology relates the qualities of a disorder to those of a remedy. Taste matters, alongside properties such as hot and cold, heavy and light, or rough and smooth. The six tastes are sweet, sour, salty, bitter, pungent, and astringent. In this reasoning, qualities that intensify a disturbance may be avoided, while opposing qualities may be selected to counter it. [Arya, *Tibetan Materia Medica*](https://www.tibetanmedicine-edu.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/materia_medica.pdf)

For example, a practitioner may explain a warming, nourishing preparation as a response to a condition characterized as cold, light, or rough. This is an illustration of the framework, rather than a prescription for anyone who feels cold or anxious. The same everyday adjective can carry a more specific meaning within a diagnostic system.

The explanatory step is worth making explicit: a medicine fits a pattern because its qualities are understood to modify that pattern. That gives the intervention a rationale. Whether it produces the predicted benefit, in a defined population and without unacceptable harm, remains an empirical question.

### Why the preparation matters

“Tibetan medicine” is too broad a label for a clinical experiment. Even a formula name may not identify a fully standardized product. Schwabl and van der Valk describe how formulas can be adapted to regional plants and local conditions, with different botanical materials occupying related traditional roles. Their discussion is a perspective on pharmaceutical practice, rather than proof that those substitutions are clinically equivalent. [Schwabl and van der Valk, 2019](https://journals.ed.ac.uk/himalaya/article/view/7885)

This creates a productive research problem. Preserving a tradition's way of recognizing a medicine and establishing a reproducible research material are both serious tasks. A study needs enough detail about species, plant parts, processing, composition, and batch quality for someone else to know what was tested.

I am open to the possibility that a multi-ingredient preparation acts through several pathways. Complexity alone, however, cannot establish beneficial synergy. The proposed combination needs to be compared with meaningful alternatives. A pathway diagram or a plausible interaction is a starting hypothesis; a patient's outcome is a different kind of evidence.

## 6. Buddhist healing, compassion, and meditation

### The Medicine Buddha is part of the explanation

Buddhism cannot be treated as ornamental background to this subject. Men-Tsee-Khang presents the **Medicine Buddha**, *Sangye Menla* or *Bhaiṣajyaguru*, as a teacher of medicine. His blue form, medicinal plant, and bowl bring healing substances and spiritual aspiration into one image. The institution also publishes a hymn and mantra associated with him. [Men-Tsee-Khang, *Medicine Buddha*](https://mentseekhang.org/medicine-buddha/)

Namgyal Monastery's own account of the Medicine Buddha mandala describes prayers and blessings directed toward freedom from illness and suffering. This is a religious account of healing, whose meaning includes compassionate intention and liberation. Presenting it faithfully requires saying what practitioners believe it can do. It also requires identifying that belief as a religious claim, rather than presenting it as a demonstrated treatment effect. [Namgyal Monastery, *Medicine Buddha Mandala*](https://namgyalmonastery.org/mandala-of-medicine-buddha/)

One question I take from this material concerns the physician's purpose. What does it mean to approach another person's suffering with an explicit discipline of compassion? Even before considering a ritual's proposed effects, that is a substantial ethical question. It concerns how care is offered, how vulnerability is met, and what kind of relationship a patient is invited into.

### Does meditation belong here?

Yes, with some distinctions. Meditation covers different practices. Training attention, cultivating compassion, reciting a mantra, and working with the subtle body have different instructions and aims. Some institutions teach the *Yuthok Nyingthig*, a Vajrayana practice tradition associated with Tibetan medicine, as a spiritual counterpart to medical learning. That does not make every Tibetan prescription a meditation treatment, or every physician's practice identical. [Sowa Rigpa Institute](https://www.sowarigpainstitute.org/sowa-rigpa-institute)

Modern experiments provide narrower, interesting questions:

| Study | What was investigated | What it can reasonably tell us |
|---|---|---|
| Lutz and colleagues, 2004 | EEG activity in eight experienced Tibetan Buddhist practitioners and ten novice controls during a compassion-related practice | The groups differed in measured neural activity. The small, nonrandomized comparison cannot establish a clinical benefit or separate training from all background differences. |
| Weng and colleagues, 2013 | Two weeks of compassion training versus an active reappraisal comparison in healthy adults | The main analysis found greater monetary redistribution to someone treated unfairly. This concerns a measured social behavior and associated neural responses, rather than recovery from disease. |

*Table 2. Two original experiments, with their scope kept explicit.* [Lutz et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0407401101); [Weng et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797612469537)

These findings interest me because they turn aspects of mental training into questions that can be investigated. But a change in brain activity is not itself a measure of health, and generosity in an experimental task is not a clinical endpoint.

Research on meditation and mindfulness more broadly suggests benefits for some symptoms and conditions, with substantial variation in study quality and interventions. These results cannot be transferred wholesale to a particular Tibetan ritual. Safety also deserves attention: unpleasant or harmful experiences are reported, and the evidence does not support treating every practice as risk-free. [NCCIH, *Meditation and Mindfulness*](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/meditation-and-mindfulness-effectiveness-and-safety)

I would therefore discuss meditation as a family of practices with psychological, ethical, and sometimes clinical relevance. Its spiritual purposes deserve an explanation of their own, even where the available experiment measures something much smaller.

## 7. What does evidence of benefit actually look like?

A concrete example helps. A 2016 trial evaluated **Tong Luo Hua Shi (TLHS)**, a modernized formulation derived from a Tibetan preparation, in 236 people with rheumatoid arthritis. Participants were randomized to three doses or placebo for eight weeks. In the full analysis, 76.3% of the highest-dose group and 54.2% of the placebo group met ACR20, a composite improvement threshold: a difference of **22.1 percentage points**. ACR20 is not remission. [Liu et al., 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13063-016-1481-3)

The result is a signal of short-term benefit worth investigating. Stable background medications were permitted, and rescue treatment was allowed. The trial was registered after recruitment had finished. Its duration and size cannot establish long-term safety or prevention of joint damage; adverse reactions were also reported. These qualifications belong beside the result, rather than hidden after a broad claim that the treatment is “proven.” [Liu et al., 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13063-016-1481-3)

This example changes how I frame the question. Asking whether Tibetan medicine “works” bundles together too many interventions and outcomes. A more informative question identifies the preparation, the patient group, the comparison, the outcome, and the follow-up period.

It also leaves two logical possibilities open. A preparation could help even if its traditional explanation is incomplete. A compelling traditional explanation could accompany an intervention that does not help. An efficacy result and an explanation of mechanism need evidence that connects them.

### Taking the whole person seriously

One attraction of Tibetan medicine is its sustained attention to relationships among daily living, mental life, and bodily symptoms. Modern health research also examines such relationships. NCCIH's account of whole-person health explicitly includes biological, behavioral, social, and environmental factors. Chronic stress, poor sleep, and other behaviors can affect multiple areas of health. [NCCIH, *Whole Person Health*](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/whole-person-health-what-it-is-and-why-its-important)

It would therefore be misleading to describe modern medicine as inherently interested only in an isolated symptom. The WHO's integrated, people-centered care framework likewise calls for services organized around people's comprehensive needs and continuity of care. Fragmented services are a real problem; they are also a recognized problem within modern health care. [WHO, *Integrated People-Centred Care*](https://www.who.int/health-topics/integrated-people-centered-care)

The comparison I find useful concerns **how a system turns attention into action**. Which connections does it ask the clinician to notice? Which decisions follow? What can be measured afterward? A broad view of the patient becomes more valuable when we can show how it improves the care that person receives.

## 8. Safety belongs inside the account of healing

Some Tibetan preparations include processed mineral ingredients. *Tsothel*—also spelled *tsotel*—is a mercury-containing preparation associated with certain medicines. Its processing and chemical form matter to the safety question, but a traditional description of purification cannot substitute for toxicological assessment.

A human cross-sectional study compared 50 users of Tsothel-containing medicines, 50 users of other Tibetan medicines, and 20 healthy referents. Many measures did not show the hypothesized pattern of mercury toxicity. Yet the groups were not randomized, there was no pretreatment baseline, and control urine samples were lost. Recruitment after treatment had begun also limited assessment of early problems. These findings offer information about the people studied; they cannot certify every preparation or exclude delayed and uncommon harms. [Sallon et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5384498/)

An animal experiment makes the distinction sharper. Rats received Tsothel for 180 days, followed by 120 days without treatment. Investigators did not find overt kidney toxicity on the reported standard measures, but mercury remained elevated in kidney tissue after withdrawal. This is animal evidence, not a prediction of a particular human outcome. It demonstrates why tissue exposure and observable injury are separate measurements. [Xiang et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6300085/)

Neither a dramatic warning about “mercury” alone nor a blanket assurance about processing is an adequate assessment. The research question needs the chemical form, dose, duration, product quality, and characteristics of the user. A reassuring finding on one measure should not erase an unresolved question on another.

For a reader, I would compress this into three checks:

| Claim encountered | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| “This remedy has been used for centuries.” | What do we know about the particular preparation and its outcomes, beyond its history of use? |
| “A study showed it works.” | Was it a laboratory experiment, an observational study, or a controlled human trial—and what outcome changed? |
| “It is natural or traditionally purified.” | What product-specific evidence addresses composition, exposure, interactions, and harms over the relevant period? |

*Table 3. Questions I would carry from this reading into any further claim about treatment.*

Recognizing the limits of these studies does not require dismissing the people who developed or use the medicines. It means taking the consequences of treatment seriously enough to investigate them. Decisions about actual treatment also need clinical assessment; a reading-based overview cannot establish whether a preparation is appropriate for an individual or justify replacing effective care.

## 9. What stays with me

The more I read, the less useful “ancient wisdom” became as an explanation. It compresses too much: a medical curriculum, local plant knowledge, philosophical commitments, skilled observation, and unanswered empirical questions.

This is also a living relationship with particular places. A 2025 field study in Ladakh's Sapi Valley documented 126 plant species, 55 recognized for medicinal applications in Sowa Rigpa, and described pressures on habitats and plant populations. Such work records knowledge and its ecological setting; it does not demonstrate that every recorded use is effective. [Gurmet et al., 2025](https://nopr.niscpr.res.in/handle/123456789/66560)

That distinction matters to me. The value of preserving a vocabulary, a craft, or a relationship with an environment need not depend on proving every therapeutic proposition attached to it. Conversely, cultural value cannot decide a question about clinical benefit or toxicity.

What I take most seriously from Tibetan medicine is its effort to connect bodily life with habits, emotions, surroundings, and the purpose of care. I want to understand the specificity of that effort: how its categories guide attention, what practitioners learn to recognize, and where a judgment can be revised. The language of balance becomes interesting when we can follow it into a concrete decision.

Expectation, trust, and the treatment encounter may also contribute through placebo and contextual effects, although that possibility cannot explain every reported improvement or identify a medicine's specific action. [NCCIH, *Placebo Effect*](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/placebo-effect)

My position after this reading is one of informed curiosity. I want traditional explanations presented clearly enough to be understood, and treatment claims stated precisely enough to be examined. A person seeking help deserves both the breadth of attention that makes them feel seen and the care with evidence that makes treatment accountable.
